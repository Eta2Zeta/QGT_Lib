Case1: J = Jx , kz = π
So on the plane kz = π,
                             π                π
                       cos     = 0,     sin     = 1,    sin π = 0.
                             2                2
Therefore

                      tx = 0,      tz = 0,        λx = 0,    λy = 0
and only λz survives. Thus if you turn on only Jx , the Hamiltonian on kz = π
reduces to

                    H (kz = π) = ε0,k + λz (k)τy σz + Jx τz σx
Now comes the key point. Do a spin rotation about the x-axis by π/2. Under
that rotation,

                                 σx → σx ,        σz → −σy
Concretely, we do unitary transformation on the Hamiltonian with the operator:
U = √12 (I − iσx ). With the basic pauli matrix algebra, we find:
                                                       
                           1                  1
              U σx U † = √ (I − iσx ) σx √ (I + iσx ) = σx ,
                            2                  2
and
                                 1
                   U σz U † =      (I − iσx ) σz (I + iσx ) = −σy .
                                 2
So the Hamiltonian becomes

                        H ′ = ε0,k − λz (k)τy σy + Jx τz σx
But τy σy is real (imaginary × imaginary), and τz σx is also real, so H ′ (k) is a
real matrix for every (kx , ky ) on that plane. That means there is an antiunitary


                                              1
symmetry acting like complex conjugation on the whole kz = π plane. In
other words, after acting on the Hamiltonian with the complex conjugation, the
Hamiltonian is unchanged. But the Berry curvature changes sign under complex
conjugation while the momentum point is unchanged (see Appendix A), it must
satisfy

                               Ωxy (k) = −Ωxy (k),
where Ωxy (k), is the Berry curvature of H ′ and −Ωxy (k) is the Berry curvature
of H ′ after complex conjugation. But the Berry curvature is real, so it must be
zero on that plane:
                                  Ωxy (k) = 0
everywhere on that plane, except possibly at singular band-touching points.

Case 2: J = Jz , kz = 0
On the plane where kz = 0, with only Jz turned on, the g-wave altermagnet
Hamiltonian reduces to

                  H = ε0 + t3 τx + λx τy σx + λy τy σy + Jz τz σz .
Similar to above, we will perform a combination of a unitary transformation
with a complex conjugation. Let the unitary operation to be

                                     U = τx σx .

Acting on λx τy σx , τx anticommutes with τy and σx commutes with σx , we get
−λx τy σx . For λy τy σy , τx anticommutes with τy and σx anticommutes with
σy , we get λy τy σy . Then finally for Jz τz σz , τx anticommutes with τz and σx
anticommutes with σz , we get Jz τz σz . Therefore, after the transformation the
Hamiltonian becomes:

                 H ′ = ε0 + t3 τx − λx τy σx + λy τy σy + Jz τz σz .
Since −λx τy σx is the only imaginary term, complex conjugation flips it back
and we get back H, and the same condition on the Berry curvature:

                               Ωxy (k) = −Ωxy (k).
And therefore
                                    Ωxy (k) = 0.
